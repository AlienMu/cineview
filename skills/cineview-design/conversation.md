# Interview the user before building

Keep interviewing the user about the page until you both picture the same page. Work down the
decision tree below, settling first the decisions that others depend on. Ask one question at a
time and give your recommended answer with each one. If the answer is already in what the user
sent, look it up instead of asking. Write no page files until the user has confirmed the
storyboard (`SKILL.md`).

The questions are about the user's page, and the user supplies the answers. Your
recommendations come from their material and from the references in this folder, and the user
is free to take them, change them or say something else.

## How to ask

- **One question per message.** Wait for the answer before you ask the next one.
- **A recommended answer with every question, and its basis**: what in their product, readers or
  material led you to it. "Yes" should then be a complete reply. Where the choice is open, add two
  or three alternatives.
- **Look before you ask.** Open the images: subject, orientation, quality, how many. Check videos
  with `ffprobe`: length, size, frame rate, keyframes. Read the copy, the current site and the
  brand files. Ask only what the material cannot tell you, and say what you found: "The clip is
  10 s and portrait; the three screenshots are frames from it."
- **Turn vague answers into a choice.** "Make it look premium" becomes: "Premium as in A, quiet
  and spacious; B, dark and glossy; or C, editorial with large serif titles? I'd suggest A,
  because your photos are bright and need room."
- **Use plain words.** Describe what a reader sees ("the photo slides in from the side as you
  scroll"), not attribute names. Use the user's language.
- **Restate each decision in one line** once it is answered, and keep a running list of decisions.
- **Go back when an answer changes an earlier branch.** A video turns up, the audience changes, a
  scene loses its material: return to that decision, say what changes, and settle it again.
- **Directions differ in kind.** When you offer directions, make them differ in structure or
  mode, not only in colour or type.

## The decision tree

Roughly in this order. Each decision depends on the ones above it.

1. **Devices.** This is the first question: phone, desktop or both. Base your recommendation on
   what the first message gives you. An invitation shared in a chat app suggests phone; a tool
   people look up at work suggests desktop; a shop or a café suggests both. If the message gives
   you nothing to go on, recommend both, as one scroll page, and say you will come back to it once
   the purpose is clear. Tell the user what each answer leads to:
   - **Phone**: the page can be drag (one screen per gesture) or scroll, designed for one hand on a
     portrait screen (`phone.md`).
   - **Desktop**: scroll. Phones still open desktop pages, so each scene also needs a narrow
     version.
   - **Both**: **A**, one scroll page for both; or **B**, the same scenes, paged by the finger on
     phones and scrolled on desktops (`phone.md`, "Both devices").
   - If you are going to offer drag, also say this: a drag page opened on a computer responds
     only to dragging with the mouse, and to the arrow keys after a click. The wheel and the
     trackpad do nothing.
2. **Purpose and reader.** What the page is for and who reads it.
3. **The one thing.** What a reader should remember, or do, after the page.
4. **Material.** What exists (look first), what is missing, and which ideas that rules out.
   `sources.md` has questions that draw a look and motion out of the product itself.
5. **Page mode.** A page has one input, so this is decided for the whole page.
   - Phone: **drag** suits one idea per screen, a picture and a line, a page read like a short
     film. **Scroll** suits longer reading, many items, and readers who expect an ordinary page.
   - Both: **B** suits scenes that are one screen each and read well either way. **A** suits
     longer content and held scenes that play as the reader scrolls.
   - Desktop: scroll.
6. **Direction and number of scenes.** Offer two or three directions. Each has its number of
   scenes and one line per scene. Recommend one. How to arrive at the scenes:
   - Start from what the reader should take away. One scene per point, plus an opening and a
     close, is a common starting count, not a rule.
   - Then let the material reshape that count. A single long held scene can carry a whole demo.
     Eight strong photos may want one screen each. A scene with nothing real to show tends to
     become decoration.
   - Keep drag pages short: every scene costs the reader a gesture.
   - Contrast helps a sequence. A held scene reads as a held scene when there is ordinary scroll
     around it, and a quiet scene makes the next loud one louder.
7. **Each scene, one at a time**, as its own small branch:
   1. **Point**: the one thing this scene says.
   2. **What the reader sees.** Give two or three options, plainly described, and pick one. Draw
      them from how the words and pictures relate (`relationships.md`), the motion and what it
      says (`motion.md`), and the treatment the material suits (`images.md`, `video.md`,
      `type.md`, `line-art.md`).
   3. **Kind of scene.** Scroll pages: an ordinary section, a held scene the scroll plays
      through, a sideways row, or cards that stack. Drag pages: every scene is one screen, so the
      choice is its composition.
   4. **The handover.**
      - Scroll: how this scene meets the next, whether a plain continuation, a cover or a held
        stretch (`handovers.md`).
      - Drag: ask **"how do we change into this scene?"** The options are push, cover, joining
        at the boundary, or a palette change past the middle. For each, say what the reader's
        finger brings in first, what plays after release, and what runs backwards when they
        drag back (`drag.md`).
   5. **Material**: which of theirs it uses, what it still needs, and what stands in if that
      never arrives. The scene might be left out, drawn, or given a marked placeholder.
   6. **Words**: theirs, or two or three options (a title and a sentence) in each language.
   7. **Both devices**: how the scene reads on a phone. On a B page, also how it reads as a drag
      screen.
8. **Look.** A visual style from `styles.md` or from their brand, with what goes with it
   (`pairings.md`). Colours can come from their own material (`colour.md`). A style is not a
   direction: the directions in decision 6 differ in structure or mode, and any of them can take
   any style.
9. **Constraints.** Languages, motion preferences, accessibility needs, things it must not do,
   the deadline.
10. **Storyboard and material list**, confirmed by the user.

## What a question looks like

> **Scene 2 of 5: what should it show?**
> I'd suggest **one photo per screen, filling it, with its caption sliding in from the left**.
> Your eight photos are strong enough to carry the page alone.
> Other options: **B**, the photo beside its caption, alternating sides; **C**, all eight in a
> sideways row.
> Reply "yes", pick B or C, or tell me what you have in mind.

A drag scene's handover:

> **Changing into scene 3: how should it arrive?**
> I'd suggest **the new photo covers the old one from below while the old one dims**. Your
> photos are a sequence from one evening, and a cover reads as the next moment.
> As the reader drags, the new photo rises over the old one. When they let go, the caption types
> in. If they drag back, the photo slides away again and the earlier caption returns.
> Other option: **B**, the page pushes up and the caption runs ahead of the photo.

And a direction proposal (decision 6):

> **A. Quiet gallery (5 scenes).** The photos carry the page, one per screen, with short captions.
> 1 The title over the strongest photo · 2-4 One photo each, the name and the place ·
> 5 Dates, address, tickets.
> **B. One walk through the show (3 scenes).** A single long held scene where the photos pass by
> like a corridor, then the details.
> I'd suggest A: eight strong photos and a short message need little else.

## The storyboard and the material list

Once every scene is settled, show the plan in one place:

| # | Scene | Says | Reader sees | Words | Kind | Handover | Material (have / need) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

On scroll pages, **Handover** says how this scene meets the next one. On drag pages it is the
**Change** column: how the page changes into this scene, and what runs backwards on the way back.
Pages for both devices add an **On phones** column.

Then list everything still needed, with how to prepare it:
- **Video**: 5-15 s, at the aspect it will show at, no sound needed. If it plays as the reader
  scrolls or drags, every frame should be a keyframe (`video.md`).
- **Screenshots**: about twice the width they will show at, all from one device or browser size,
  with real content.
- **Photos**: how many, landscape or portrait, in similar light if they show as a set. Portrait
  crops for phone pages.
- **Numbers, quotes, logos**: real ones with their source. Logos as SVG.
- **Words**: approximate lengths, such as a title of two to six words and a sentence of ten to
  twenty-five.
- **Brand**: colours as hex values, font names or files.

## When it is done

The interview is finished when:
- every scene has its point, what the reader sees, its kind, its handover, its material and its
  words agreed;
- the material list says what is still missing and how to prepare it;
- the user has said yes to the storyboard in their own words.

Then build. Show the page early, as a link or a screenshot of each scene. Route later changes
through the storyboard first, then the page (`SKILL.md`, "Scope").

## When the user wants it fast

If the user says "just make it" or "you decide", fill in the tree with your recommendations,
mark each one as assumed, and show the storyboard and the material list in one message. Build
once the user says yes to it.
